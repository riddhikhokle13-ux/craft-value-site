Research model: Pipili, Odisha

Can better signalling, market access and producer participation actually raise how much value reaches the artisan; not just the enterprise?

This page is based on fieldwork with artisans, traders and consumers in Pipili's appliqué craft cluster. It turns that fieldwork into a working model, so the trade-offs behind the question above become visible rather than assumed.

02What the research found

A craft with real demand, but a value chain that doesn't pass much of it back.

Pipili's appliqué work locally known as 'Chandua' supplies both the Jagannath Temple's ritual textiles and a large tourist retail trade along the Puri-Konark road. The fieldwork behind this model looked at all three sides of that trade: the people making it, the people selling it, and the people buying it.

"A traditional piece requiring three to four hours of intricate hand-stitching frequently retails for only ₹70, leaving the artisan with a mere ₹5 to ₹10 profit margin. Large, air-conditioned showrooms often purchase these same items cheaply and resell them for ₹500 to ₹700." Shopkeeper and artisan, interviewed in Konark, 24 February 2026

This is one of the field observations, not a market-wide average, it doesn't apply to every transaction in Pipili. It's used here because it's the clearest anchor for the question the whole model is built to test: where does the value made by a couple hours of skilled hand-stitching actually end up?

A few more findings behind the model

71.4% of artisans learned the craft through family, and the remaining 28.6% work on it independently, the craft is still substantially transmitted intergenerationally.

The majority of artisans report earning ₹10,000 - ₹20,000 a month from appliqué work; a further part-time cohort earns up to ₹5,000, irregularly.

58.8% of traders reported declining sales or profitability over the previous ten years.

63.6% of surveyed consumers report that they check whether a piece is genuinely handmade before buying it.

Full sample details, methods and limitations are in section 13.

03The Model

Every input in the following simulator traces back to something observed in Pipili, and every output is calculated live from those inputs.

Fieldwork
Observations from Pipili.Interviews, surveys and focus groups with artisans, traders and consumers, plus direct observation of workshops, showrooms and the temple.
Findings
What those observations suggest.Patterns like tourist concentration, thin artisan margins, low GI awareness, and a real consumer appetite for verified handwork.
Model
Relationships to variables.12 inputs covering market channels, pricing, cost structure and how value is shared with the producer chosen because the fieldwork gave a reason to include them, and no others.
Scenario
What changesPrice, channel mix, authenticity positioning, producer share, and so on, one variable or several at once.
Outcome
Model calculationsRevenue, margin, break-even, producer income, income per hour, and how exposed the business is to a demand shock.
Interpretation
What it means, and what it doesn't.Every outcome is shown next to a note on the mechanism driving it.

Three labels recur throughout this page: field observed for something the fieldwork measured directly, model derived for something the simulator calculates, and model assumption for a parameter introduced to make the scenario model run where the fieldwork alone can't determine a number. Keeping these separate is the point.

08The signal, and why it isn't landing

Is the value of authenticity actually reaching the customer?

Pipili appliqué has held Geographical Indication protection since 2008; a legal signal of authenticity and origin. In practice, that signal breaks down well before it reaches the point of sale.

    13Methodology

    Where the evidence came from, and what it can't tell us

    Fieldwork

    Five days, Pipili, Bhubaneswar, Konark, and Puri, Odisha.

    Participants

    21 artisans · 17 traders · 11 consumers · two women-artisan focus groups (~20 participants)

    Methods

    Structured surveys, semi-structured interviews, focus groups, observation, and documentary interviews.

    The interactive model does not claim to statistically represent all artisan enterprises, in Pipili or elsewhere. It uses this fieldwork as an empirical foundation for exploring relationships and testing scenarios. A tool for thinking with the evidence, not a forecast.

    Limitations

    • Small, purposive/snowball sample. 21 artisans, 17 traders, 11 consumers. Network bias is likely, and the findings are indicative of this fieldwork, not statistically representative of Pipili or of Indian artisans generally.
    • Five-day fieldwork window. Seasonal effects, including the well-documented Rath Yatra demand spike, are described qualitatively but not measured.
    • Small consumer sample. Any price/demand relationship tied to authenticity in this model is a transparent, editable assumption for exploration. Not a statistically estimated elasticity.
    • Authenticity is not assumed economically superior. The underlying report documents a real tension between authenticity, affordability, market adaptation and livelihood survival; this model exposes that tension rather than resolving it in either direction.
    • Correlation, not established causation. The Value Signal Gap places GI awareness, trader communication and consumer verification side by side. It does not claim that closing one gap causes the others to move.
    • This is a Pipili-specific model. It is intentionally not a generic India-wide artisan platform; its parameters shouldn't be read onto other craft clusters without new fieldwork.

    Model assumptions

    Everything below is a setting the model needs in order to run. Each is editable; changes apply immediately across every section.

    What each assumption does, and why it's illustrative rather than field-derived

    Base addressable demand : sets the monthly unit volume the model scales from. Illustrative: the fieldwork did not measure total addressable market size.

    Fixed costs : workshop, tools and overhead the enterprise must cover before profit. Illustrative placeholder, editable to match a real cost structure.

    Price sensitivity : how much modelled demand falls as price rises above the reference point. Illustrative; no price experiment was run in the field.

    Repeat-purchase boost : how much a higher repeat rate lifts modelled demand.

    Authenticity → demand / WTP bonuses : the two assumptions behind section 07. Directionally motivated by the 72.7% finding, but the size of the effect is illustrative, not estimated.

    Women's baseline share / pay multiplier :the baseline (~12%) reflects fieldwork description of women's limited access to cutting, design and sales; the multiplier is a stated, editable assumption about how much better-paid those tasks are, not a measured wage figure.

    —A note from the researcher

    Why a model, and not just a report

    I went to Pipili wanting to understand something specific: why a craft with clear consumer demand and real cultural protection still leaves the people making it with so little. The report answers that with evidence. This page exists because a number like "producer value share: 16%" is easy to write and easy to skim past. But it's harder to skim past once you've moved the slider yourself and watched producer income and enterprise margin pull in opposite directions.

    A model like this can show a trade-off clearly. It cannot tell you which side of that trade-off is right, and it cannot substitute for the small sample and short fieldwork window behind it; both are stated plainly in section 13. What it can do is turn "artisans are underpaid" from a conclusion into a mechanism that can be tested.
